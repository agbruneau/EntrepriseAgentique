# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This document presents a comprehensive, meta-scientifically rigorous literature review examining the interoperability and orchestration of Large Language Model (LLM) agents in enterprise contexts, anchored at an August 15, 2026 baseline (192 items) and extended via longitudinal verification and literature update passes on October 29 and November 1, 2026. Rather than synthesizing preprint claims at face value, the author first audits the evidentiary regime, peer-review attestation, version stability, and measurement instrument validity of the corpus across ten thematic fronts: protocols, security, identity and delegation, multi-agent systems, evaluation and observability, transactions, business processes, governance, the agentic Web, and choreography/swarms. The review unifies these disparate subfields around a central architectural diagnosis—that current agentic stacks fail to separate what is authorized from what is merely present across content, authority, and effect—while testing three specific technology-watch hypotheses against the academic record.

## Strengths
* The review introduces a highly original meta-scientific methodology by auditing peer-review attestation, longitudinal preprint revision drift, and measurement instrument validity before synthesizing domain findings, effectively preventing enterprise architects from grounding decisions on unverified headline metrics or flawed static scanners.
* The cross-layer architectural synthesis in Section 14.1 provides a clarifying design invariant by demonstrating that failures across protocols, prompt security, identity delegation, and workflow execution all stem from conflating ambient presence with explicit authorization across three dimensions: content, authority, and effect.
* The analysis of multi-hop delegation and observability convincingly establishes that the inability to reconstruct mandate chains post-hoc from standard telemetry is a deficit of execution-time cryptographic binding and standardization rather than a lack of conceptual vocabulary.
* The longitudinal re-audits and Crossref control passes transparently quantify how repository metadata under-reports peer review, establishing a strict, verifiable lower bound of refereed literature while shifting the evidentiary weight to the specific verification regime of each individual claim.

## Weaknesses
* The methodological decision to rely primarily on LLM agents reading only arXiv abstracts and metadata without inspecting full texts undermines construct validity, as abstracts routinely omit critical methodological caveats, sample biases, and synthetic calibration disclosures that only appear in the body text.
* The characterization of type-directed privilege separation in the security synthesis overlooks foundational prior art and dynamic benchmarks that already quantified the utility trade-offs of capability-based control and data-flow isolation, and similarly omits classic distributed systems baselines for append-only attenuated capability chains and normative provenance/token standards (Reference Paper: Defeating Prompt Injections by Design, Edoardo Debenedetti et al., 2025; Reference Paper: AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Benchmark Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents, Edoardo Debenedetti et al., 2024; Reference Paper: Macaroons: Cookies with Contextual Caveats for Decentralized Authorization in the Cloud, Arnar Birgisson et al., 2014; Reference Paper: W3C PROV-DM: The PROV Data Model, Luc Moreau and Paolo Missier, 2013; Reference Paper: IETF RFC 8693: OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange, Michael B. Jones et al., 2020).
* While the Crossref control pass is framed as a conservative lower bound, relying exclusively on exact title matching without querying OpenReview or PMLR systematically undercounts peer-reviewed machine learning and systems papers and misses works whose titles changed upon publication.
* The manuscript contains several internal consistency, reproducibility, and formatting issues—including omitted search queries and candidate screening counts for the primary pass, an assigned reference ([78]) missing from its home section, unapplied errata in the main text, truncated comment strings in the bibliography, and severe text collisions between DOI and date columns in Table 14.

2. **Potential Concern Regarding the Framing and Methodological Boundary of the Conformance Checking Gap Across Sections 10.1, 10.3, and 13.1–13.2 (Pages 19–21 and 25):**
   In Section 10.3 (*Ce que la littérature ne traite pas*, pp. 20–21), the review establishes a strict negative finding: *"Personne ne mesure la conformité d’exécution d’un agent à un modèle de processus. Le contrôle de conformité au sens de la discipline — aligner une trace réelle contre un modèle de référence, quantifier fitness et précision — n’est appliqué à aucune trace d’agent autonome."* There are three closely related methodological and conceptual considerations regarding how this gap is bounded in the main text:
   - **Narrow API Verification Query:** Section 10.3 substantiates the absence of such work by reporting a single exact-phrase arXiv API query (`"conformance checking" AND "LLM"`), which returned seven entries as of August 15, 2026. In the Business Process Management (BPM) and multi-agent communities—which Section 3.2 (p. 8) and Table 14 (pp. 51–52) note publish heavily in Springer LNBIP/LNCS proceedings and Elsevier's *Information Systems* rather than exclusively on arXiv—studies evaluating autonomous execution traces against formal process specifications often pair `"conformance checking"` with `"agent"` / `"agentic"` rather than `"LLM"`, or refer to *trace alignment*, *process compliance*, or *runtime compliance verification*. (Notably, the November 1st annex on pp. 64 and 68–69 recognizes this limitation by supplementing the query with `"conformance checking" AND "agent"` and identifying Cho et al. `[230]`, which measures out-of-sample trace replay *fitness* $\ge 0.997$ against a discovered automaton without using the `"conformance checking"` keyword.) Explicitly noting in Section 10.3 that this query is an illustrative indicator alongside the broader front search (Section 2.1), rather than a sufficient proof of absence on its own, would strengthen the methodological rigor of the claim.
   - **Post-Hoc Conformance Checking vs. Constructive Runtime Enforcement:** In Section 10.1 (p. 19) and Section 10.2 (p. 20), the review notes that architectures such as CHAI `[137]` subordinate the language model to a formal DCR graph controller that encodes legal obligations and drives the dialogue, and `[134]` turns human-approval gates into enforceable protocol invariants. In formal process management, a DCR graph or state-machine controller acting as an active runtime execution harness enforces 100% trace fitness with respect to the normative specification by construction (preventing non-conformant transitions from executing). Explicitly distinguishing in Section 10.3 between *a posteriori* conformance checking of unconstrained or loosely orchestrated agent traces against a prescribed *a priori* business model (where fitness and precision are indeed unmeasured) and *a priori* runtime enforcement via formal controllers like `[137]` or `[134]` would sharpen the conceptual precision of the gap.
   - **Cross-Front Boundary with Formal Protocol Verification:** In Section 13.1 and Section 13.2 (p. 25), the review discusses `[171]` (*TraceFix*, which checks and repairs agent execution traces against TLA+ protocol specifications and uses a finite-state runtime monitor to reject out-of-topology operations) and `[172]` (which evaluates LTL-based finite-state monitors against agent execution trajectories).
